Sum Rule

If a task can be performed by choosing exactly one of several mutually exclusive options, the total number of ways is the sum of the ways for each option.

Rule

|T| = |T1| + |T2| + ··· + |Tn|
Condition The options must be disjoint—no outcome may belong to more than one option.

Example

Choose exactly one movie

6Indian movies
3Hollywood movies

The categories are disjoint, so their choices are added.

6 + 3 = 9 ways
02

OR vs AND

For 6 Indian movies and 3 Hollywood movies, use addition for an OR choice and multiplication for an AND choice. Correct the count when the sets overlap.

Case Rule Calculation Answer
OR, no common movie Add 6 + 3 9
AND, no common movie Multiply 6 × 3 18
OR, 1 common movie Add and subtract the overlap 6 + 3 − 1 8
AND, 1 common movie Multiply; remove (X, X) when two distinct movies are required 6 × 3 − 1 17
OR → Add
AND → Multiply

Overlap: Correct any double or invalid counting. In the final case, the answer is 17 only when two different movies must be chosen. If the common movie may serve as both choices simultaneously, the count remains 18.

Four-Letter Words

Question: How many four-letter words can be formed from the 26-letter English alphabet when:

  1. repetition is allowed?
  2. repetition is not allowed?
  3. repetition is allowed, but not consecutively?
a.

Repetition allowed: Each of the four positions has 26 choices.

26 × 26 × 26 × 26 = 264

456,976
b.

No repetition: The number of choices decreases after each position.

26 × 25 × 24 × 23 = 26P4

358,800
c.

No consecutive repetition: The first position has 26 choices; each later position can use any letter except the immediately preceding one.

26 × 25 × 25 × 25 = 26(253)

406,250

Interpretation of (c): Non-adjacent repetition is allowed, such as ABAC; identical adjacent letters, such as the AA in AABC, are not allowed.

04

Counting Matrices

Question: Let n ≥ 2 and let S1 be the set of all n × n matrices whose entries belong to {a, b, c}. Find |S1|.

1.

An n × n matrix contains n2 independent positions.

2.

Each position has 3 choices: a, b, or c.

3.

By the product rule, multiply 3 once for every matrix position.

3 × 3 × ··· × 3  (n2 times)

|S1| = 3n²

Key idea: Number of matrices = (choices per entry)number of entries.

Subtraction Rule

When two sets overlap, add their sizes and subtract the intersection once to ensure that each element is counted exactly once.

Inclusion–Exclusion for Two Sets

|A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|
Why subtract? Elements in A ∩ B are included once in |A| and once in |B|, so they are initially counted twice.

Memory Rule

Add both sets, then remove one copy of the overlap.

Every element in the union is then counted exactly once.

Add − Overlap

Question: How many bit strings of length 8 either start with 1 or end with 00?

A. 32    B. 128    C. 160    D. 192

A

Strings starting with 1: The first bit is fixed; the remaining 7 bits are free.

|A| = 27 = 128

B

Strings ending with 00: The final 2 bits are fixed; the first 6 bits are free.

|B| = 26 = 64

A∩B

Strings satisfying both: The first bit and final 2 bits are fixed; the middle 5 bits are free.

|A ∩ B| = 25 = 32

∴

Apply the subtraction rule.

|A ∪ B| = 128 + 64 − 32

160 — Option C
06

Complement Rule

When the desired outcomes are difficult to count directly, count all outcomes and subtract those that do not satisfy the requirement.

Rule

Desired = Total − Undesired
Set Form |A| = |U| − |Ac|, where U is the universal set and Ac is the complement of A.

When to Use

Look for phrases such as:

≥ 1at least one
Not allone or more exceptions

Counting the opposite event is often much simpler.

Question: How many strings of four lowercase English letters contain the letter x at least once?

1.

Total strings: Each of the four positions has 26 choices.

264 = 456,976

2.

Undesired strings: If x is absent, each position has 25 choices.

254 = 390,625

∴

Apply the complement rule.

264 − 254 = 456,976 − 390,625

66,351 strings

Why this works: Subtracting strings with no x leaves precisely the strings containing one or more occurrences of x.

07

Ordered Card Selection

Standard Deck Theory

A standard deck contains 52 unique cards, with no jokers. Each card is identified by one suit and one rank.

4 suits Hearts, Diamonds, Clubs, Spades
13 ranks A, 2–10, J, Q, K
26 red Hearts and Diamonds
26 black Clubs and Spades
Useful Counting Facts Each suit has 13 cards. Each rank has 4 cards—one from each suit. Therefore, the deck contains 4 aces, 12 face cards (J, Q, K), and 48 non-aces.

Question: Two cards are picked one after another from a standard 52-card deck. If order matters, how many selections contain at least one ace?

1.

Total ordered selections: There are 52 choices for the first card and 51 for the second.

52 × 51 = 2,652

2.

Selections with no ace: Choose both cards from the 48 non-aces.

48 × 47 = 2,256

∴

Use the Complement Rule: at least one ace = all selections − no-ace selections.

(52 × 51) − (48 × 47) = 2,652 − 2,256

396 ways

Assumption: Cards are drawn without replacement. As order matters, drawing Ace–King and King–Ace gives two different outcomes.

Job Assignment

Question: There are 6 jobs with distinct difficulty levels and 3 computers with distinct processing speeds. Each job is assigned to one computer such that:

  • the fastest computer receives the toughest job;
  • the slowest computer receives the easiest job; and
  • every computer receives at least one job.

Find the number of valid assignments.

1.

Assign the extreme jobs: The toughest job is fixed on the fastest computer, and the easiest job is fixed on the slowest computer.

These assignments contribute 1 × 1 way.

2.

Assign the remaining 4 jobs: Each job may independently go to any of the 3 computers.

Total assignments = 34 = 81

3.

Remove invalid assignments: The fastest and slowest computers are already nonempty. An assignment is invalid only when the middle-speed computer receives none of the remaining jobs.

Invalid assignments = 24 = 16

∴

Apply the Complement Rule.

34 − 24 = 81 − 16

65 ways

Key observation: Only the middle-speed computer can be empty after the two compulsory assignments; therefore, exclude the cases in which all four remaining jobs go only to the fastest or slowest computer.

Counting Full Houses

Question: A poker hand consists of 5 cards chosen from a standard 52-card deck. A full house contains 3 cards of one rank and 2 cards of a different rank—for example, 3 kings and 2 eights. How many different full houses are possible?

1.

Choose the rank of the triple: Any of the 13 ranks may be used.

13 choices

2.

Choose 3 suits for that rank: Each rank has 4 cards, one per suit.

C(4, 3) = 4 choices

3.

Choose the rank of the pair: It must differ from the triple's rank.

12 choices

4.

Choose 2 suits for that rank:

C(4, 2) = 6 choices

∴

Apply the Product Rule.

13 × C(4, 3) × 12 × C(4, 2) = 13 × 4 × 12 × 6

3,744 hands

No double counting: The rank appearing three times and the rank appearing twice have different roles, so every full house is counted exactly once.

Permutations with Repeated Elements

When some objects are identical, swapping identical copies does not produce a new arrangement. Divide the ordinary factorial count by the factorial of each repetition count.

Repeated-Element Formula

Number of arrangements = n! / (n1! n2! ··· nk!)
Meaning There are n total objects, with identical groups of sizes n1, n2, …, nk.

Gap Method

Use when identical objects must not be adjacent.

Arrange the other objects first, identify the gaps around them, and place at most one restricted object in each selected gap.

Question: How many distinct arrangements of the letters in TALLAHASSEE have no two A's adjacent?

1.

Count the letters: There are 11 letters: A occurs 3 times; E, L, and S occur twice each; H and T occur once each.

Total distinct arrangements = 11!/(3!2!2!2!) = 831,600

2.

Arrange the 8 non-A letters: E, E, L, L, S, S, H, T.

8!/(2!2!2!) = 5,040

3.

Choose gaps for the A's: Eight arranged letters create 9 gaps, including the two ends. Choose 3 different gaps so that no two A's are adjacent.

C(9, 3) = 84

∴

Apply the Product Rule.

[8!/(2!2!2!)] × C(9, 3) = 5,040 × 84

423,360

Gap pattern: _ X _ X _ X _ X _ X _ X _ X _ X _. Placing at most one A in a chosen gap guarantees that the A's are non-adjacent.

Binomial and Its Coefficients

The Binomial Theorem expands a power of two terms and explains each coefficient as a count of selections.

Derivation of the Binomial Expansion

(a + b)n is a product of n identical brackets:

(a + b)(a + b)···(a + b)

Every term in the expansion is formed by selecting either a or b from each bracket.

If b is selected from exactly r brackets, then a must be selected from the remaining n − r brackets. The resulting term is an−rbr.

The r brackets contributing b can be chosen in C(n,r) ways. Hence its coefficient is C(n,r).

General term: C(n,r)an−rbr
(a + b)n = Σr=0n C(n,r)an−rbr
Coefficient Symmetry C(n,r) = C(n,n−r), because choosing r brackets for b is equivalent to choosing the remaining n−r brackets for a.

Example: Expansion of (a + b)3

For the term a2b, choose one of the three brackets to contribute b. This can be done in C(3,1) = 3 ways.

(a + b)3 = C(3,0)a3 + C(3,1)a2b + C(3,2)ab2 + C(3,3)b3
(a + b)3 = a3 + 3a2b + 3ab2 + b3

Main idea: C(n,r) counts the selections of brackets that produce an−rbr.

Important Binomial Identities

Identity 1 — Sum of All Binomial Coefficients

From the Binomial Theorem,

(1+x)n = Σk=0n C(n,k)xk

= C(n,0)x0 + C(n,1)x1 + ··· + C(n,n)xn

Substitute x=1:

2n = C(n,0) + C(n,1) + C(n,2) + ··· + C(n,n)

Result: The sum of all binomial coefficients in the nth row is 2n.

Identity 2 — Even and Odd Binomial Coefficients

Again, start with

(1+x)n = Σk=0n C(n,k)xk

For n ≥ 1, substitute x=−1:

0 = C(n,0) − C(n,1) + C(n,2) − C(n,3) + ··· + (−1)nC(n,n)

Move the odd-indexed terms to the other side:

C(n,0) + C(n,2) + C(n,4) + ··· = C(n,1) + C(n,3) + C(n,5) + ···

Result: The even-indexed and odd-indexed sums are equal. Since their combined sum is 2n, each sum equals 2n−1.

Objects into Boxes

Distribution problems are classified by whether the objects and boxes are different (distinguishable) or identical (indistinguishable).

Type Objects Boxes Counting Method
DODB Different Different Each object chooses a box independently. With empty boxes allowed: kn.
IODB Identical Different Count the number placed in each labelled box using Stars and Bars.
DOIB Different Identical Distributions differing only by swapping boxes are the same. Use set partitions.
IOIB Identical Identical Only the group sizes matter. Use integer partitions.

1. DODB — Different Objects, Different Boxes

Each of the n distinct objects has k choices, one for each labelled box.

Number of distributions = kn

Condition: This formula allows boxes to remain empty. If every box must be nonempty, use onto-function counting or inclusion–exclusion.

DODB with Exact Box Sizes

Let n distinct objects be distributed among r distinct boxes, where box Bi must receive exactly ni objects and

n1 + n2 + ··· + nr = n.

Choose n1 objects for B1: C(n,n1).

Choose n2 of the remaining objects for B2: C(n−n1,n2).

Continue similarly for every box. These are successive AND choices, so multiply:

C(n,n1)C(n−n1,n2)C(n−n1−n2,n3) ···

Ways = n!/(n1!n2!···nr!)

Multinomial coefficient: Use this formula when both objects and boxes are distinct and each labelled box has a prescribed size.

Mini Example

Distribute 5 distinct students A, B, C, D, E among three distinct rooms with sizes 2, 2, and 1.

C(5,2)C(3,2)C(1,1) = 5!/(2!2!1!) = 30

DODB Q1. Four Poker Hands of Five Cards

Question: How many ways can hands of 5 cards be distributed to each of four distinct players from a standard 52-card deck?

Sol.

Choose 5 cards successively for each player; 32 cards remain undealt.

C(52,5)C(47,5)C(42,5)C(37,5)

= [52!/(5!47!)] [47!/(5!42!)] [42!/(5!37!)] [37!/(5!32!)]

52!/[(5!)432!]

Interpretation: Players are labelled, but the order of cards within each player's hand is irrelevant.

2. IODB — Identical Objects, Different Boxes

A distribution is determined by nonnegative integer solutions of x1 + x2 + ··· + xk = n.

Empty allowed: C(n+k−1, k−1)
All nonempty: C(n−1, k−1)

Method: Represent the n objects by stars and separate the k labelled boxes using k−1 bars.

Stars and Bars — Complete Logic

First consider a string containing n identical stars and m identical bars. It has n+m positions. Choosing the m bar positions (or the n star positions) gives

(n+m)!/(n!m!) = C(n+m,m) = C(n+m,n).

Stars and bars is the standard counting technique for IODB: indistinguishable objects placed into distinguishable boxes.

Suppose n identical objects are placed in r distinct boxes. If xi is the number in box i, then

x1 + x2 + ··· + xr = n.

The n stars represent the objects. The r−1 bars are boundaries separating the stars into the r labelled boxes.

**|*|**  ⇔  (B1,B2,B3) = (2,1,2)

|***|**  ⇔  (B1,B2,B3) = (0,3,2)

Thus every stars-and-bars string corresponds to exactly one distribution, including distributions with empty boxes.

Empty allowed: C(n+r−1, r−1) = C(n+r−1,n)
Every box nonempty: C(n−1, r−1)

Why the nonempty formula works: First place one object in each box. This uses r objects, leaving n−r objects to distribute freely. Hence C((n−r)+r−1,r−1)=C(n−1,r−1). This requires n ≥ r.

Connection: Stars = indistinguishable objects; bars = boundaries between distinguishable boxes. Therefore, IODB ⇔ stars and bars.

IODB Q2. Comparing Two Distributions

True or False? The number of ways to distribute 8 identical balls to 12 distinct urns is the same as the number of ways to distribute 11 identical biscuits to 9 dogs.

Eight balls into 12 urns: C(8+12−1,12−1)=C(19,11).

  1. Combination with repetition

Selecting r items from n types when repetition is allowed is equivalent to distributing r identical selections among n distinct types:

x1+x2+···+xn=r,   xi≥0  ⇒  C(n+r−1,r).

Example — Choose 4 from 6 Types

Question: Using the elements a, b, and c, how many multisets of size 5 can be created?

b y y ≥ 0

C(5+10−1,5)=C(14,5)=2002

Quick distinction: Without repetition use C(n,r); with repetition use C(n+r−1,r).

IODB Q13. Non-Decreasing Sequences

Question: How many non-decreasing sequences of length 4 can be formed from {1, 2, 3}?

A non-decreasing sequence satisfies a1≤a2≤a3≤a4. Repetition is allowed, but entries cannot decrease from left to right.

Valid examples include (1,1,2,3), (1,1,1,2), (1,1,1,1), (2,2,2,2), (1,2,2,3), (2,3,3,3), and (3,3,3,3).

(3,2,2,2) is invalid because it decreases.

Although order normally matters for sequences, the non-decreasing condition fixes the order uniquely once the number of 1s, 2s, and 3s is known.

Let x, y, and z be the respective counts of 1s, 2s, and 3s. Then

x+y+z=4,   x,y,z≥0.

This is the IODB template: 4 identical selections (stars) distributed among 3 distinct values (boxes), requiring 2 bars.

C(4+3−1,3−1)=C(6,2)=C(6,4)=15

15 sequences

General rule: The number of non-decreasing sequences of length r formed from n distinct values is C(n+r−1,r).

IODB Q14. Three-Part Compositions of 5

Question: What is the number of 3-part compositions of n=5?

A 3-part composition of 5 is an ordered triple of positive integers satisfying

a+b+c=5,   a,b,c≥1.

Remove the positivity requirement by setting a'=a−1, b'=b−1, and c'=c−1. Then a',b',c'≥0.

(a'+1)+(b'+1)+(c'+1)=5 ⇒ a'+b'+c'=2

Apply stars and bars to distribute 2 remaining units among 3 distinct positions:

C(2+3−1,3−1)=C(4,2)=6

6 compositions

The six compositions: (1,1,3), (1,2,2), (1,3,1), (2,1,2), (2,2,1), and (3,1,1).

General rule: The number of compositions of n into exactly r positive parts is C(n−1,r−1), provided n≥r.

IODB Q15. Total Number of Compositions of n

Question: How many compositions does a positive integer n have?

A composition of n may contain 1 part, 2 parts, …, or n parts.

The number of compositions of n into exactly k positive parts is C(n−1,k−1).

These cases are mutually exclusive, so the Addition Rule gives

∑k=1n C(n−1,k−1) = ∑j=0n−1 C(n−1,j) = 2n−1.

The final equality follows from the binomial identity ∑j=0m C(m,j)=2m.

2n−1 compositions

Result: Every positive integer n has exactly 2n−1 compositions.

3. DOIB — Different Objects, Identical Boxes

Only the groups of objects matter; the boxes have no labels. For example, the distributions {a} | {b,c,d} and {b,c,d} | {a} are identical.

Exactly k nonempty boxes: S(n,k)

S(n,k) is a Stirling number of the second kind: the number of ways to partition n distinct objects into k nonempty, unlabeled groups. If empty boxes are allowed, count partitions into at most k groups.

DOIB Counting Template

For any DOIB problem:

  1. Create separate cases for all possible occupancy distributions.
  2. For each distribution, first count as in the DODB template and then divide for repeated group sizes.

If the nonempty group sizes are s1,s2,…,sr, and a size occurring repeatedly has multiplicity m, then groups of that size can be interchanged in m! ways without creating a new grouping.

Number of groupings = n! / [∏i=1r(si!) · ∏t(mt!)]

Applications: The DOIB template is used to find set partitions and equivalence relations on a set. These ideas are also studied in Set Theory.

8!/(3!3!2!2!) = 280

Inclusion–Exclusion Principle (IEP)

The Inclusion–Exclusion Principle is a counting tool used to find the number of elements in the union of a finite collection of sets. It adds individual set sizes and then corrects the overcounting by alternately subtracting and adding intersections.

Union of Two and Three Sets

For two finite sets, elements in the intersection are counted twice by |A|+|B|, so subtract the intersection once:

|A ∪ B| = |A|+|B|−|A ∩ B|

For three finite sets, first add the individual sizes, subtract every pairwise intersection, and then add the triple intersection back:

|A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A|+|B|+|C|
−|A ∩ B|−|A ∩ C|−|B ∩ C|
+|A ∩ B ∩ C|

Why is the triple intersection added? An element belonging to all three sets is added three times and removed three times by the pair corrections, leaving it counted zero times. Adding the triple intersection counts it exactly once.

General Inclusion–Exclusion Formula

For finite sets A1,A2,…,An:

|⋃i=1nAi| = ∑|Ai| −∑|Ai∩Aj| +∑|Ai∩Aj∩Ak| −··· +(−1)n+1|A1∩···∩An|

= ∑k=1n (−1)k+1 ∑1≤i₁<···<iₖ≤n |Ai₁∩···∩Aiₖ|

Singles − pairs + triples − four-way intersections + ···

A k-set intersection has sign (−1)k+1. Thus the signs are positive for odd k and negative for even k.

The number of k-set intersection terms is C(n,k), because we choose which k sets participate. The numbers of terms at successive stages are therefore

C(n,1), C(n,2), C(n,3), …, C(n,n).

For 10 sets, the groups of terms have sizes C(10,1), C(10,2), C(10,3),…,C(10,10), with alternating signs.

Four-Set Expansion

|A ∪ B ∪ C ∪ D|

= (|A|+|B|+|C|+|D|)

− (|A∩B|+|A∩C|+|A∩D|+|B∩C|+|B∩D|+|C∩D|)

+ (|A∩B∩C|+|A∩B∩D|+|A∩C∩D|+|B∩C∩D|)

− |A∩B∩C∩D|.

Symmetric IEP Problems

In exam problems, do not expand a large number of sets blindly. Look for symmetry.

If every set has size a, every pairwise intersection has size b, every triple intersection has size c, and so on, then

|A1∪···∪An| = C(n,1)a−C(n,2)b+C(n,3)c−···

Exam shortcut: Group equal-sized intersections and multiply their common size by the appropriate binomial coefficient.

Counting Multiples in an Interval

The number of integers in the inclusive interval [a,b] divisible by a positive integer n is

⌊b/n⌋−⌊(a−1)/n⌋.

The number of integers from 1 through b divisible by both m and n is

⌊b/Lcm(m,n)⌋.

More generally, an intersection of divisibility sets is counted using the least common multiple of all the corresponding divisors.

IEP Q1. Multiples of 2 or 3

Question: How many integers from 1 to 100 are multiples of 2 or 3?

Let D2 and D3 be the sets of multiples of 2 and 3.

|D2|=⌊100/2⌋=50,   |D3|=⌊100/3⌋=33

The intersection contains multiples of Lcm(2,3)=6.

|D2∩D3|=|D6|=⌊100/6⌋=16

|D2∪D3|=50+33−16=67

67 integers

IEP Q2. Neither a Multiple of 2 nor 9

Question: How many integers from 1 through 100 are neither multiples of 2 nor multiples of 9?

By De Morgan's law, D2c∩D9c=(D2∪D9)c. Therefore, subtract the union from the 100 available integers.

|D2∪D9|=⌊100/2⌋+⌊100/9⌋−⌊100/Lcm(2,9)⌋

=50+11−⌊100/18⌋=50+11−5=56

Neither = 100−56=44

44 integers

Derangements

A derangement is a permutation in which no element remains in its original position. Equivalently, a derangement is a permutation with no fixed points. The number of derangements of n distinct objects is denoted by Dn (also written !n).

Example: Derangements of Three Letters

Start with the original arrangement abc, where a, b, and c occupy positions 1, 2, and 3. There are 3!=6 permutations.

Permutation Letters in original positions Derangement?
abca, b, cNo
acbaNo
baccNo
bcaNoneYes
cabNoneYes
cbabNo

D3=2:   bca, cab

Complete Derivation of D4 Using Inclusion–Exclusion

Consider the original arrangement ABCD. A derangement must satisfy

A≠position 1,   B≠position 2,   C≠position 3,   D≠position 4.

Begin with all 4!=24 permutations. Define EA as the event that A is fixed in its original position, and define EB,EC,ED similarly. We want permutations belonging to none of these events.

Step 1 — subtract permutations with one specified element fixed.

If A is fixed, the remaining three letters can be permuted in 3!=6 ways. The same is true for each of the four letters.

C(4,1)3!=4·6=24

Step 2 — add permutations with two specified elements fixed.

Choose the two fixed letters in C(4,2) ways and permute the other two in 2! ways. These permutations were subtracted twice, so add them back.

C(4,2)2!=6·2=12

Step 3 — subtract permutations with three specified elements fixed.

Choose the three fixed letters in C(4,3) ways. Once three are fixed, the last letter is forced into its original position, giving 1!=1 arrangement for each choice.

C(4,3)1!=4

Step 4 — add the permutation with all four elements fixed.

C(4,4)0!=1

Applying the alternating inclusion–exclusion signs:

D4=4!−C(4,1)3!+C(4,2)2!−C(4,3)1!+C(4,4)0!

D4=24−24+12−4+1=9

D4 = 9

The nine derangements: BADC, BCDA, BDAC, CADB, CDAB, CDBA, DABC, DCAB, and DCBA.

General Derangement Formula

For n objects, choose which k objects are fixed and permute the remaining n−k objects. Inclusion–exclusion gives

Dn=n!−C(n,1)(n−1)!+C(n,2)(n−2)!−···+(−1)nC(n,n)0!

Since C(n,k)(n−k)!=n!/k!, this simplifies to

Dn=n! [1−1/1!+1/2!−1/3!+···+(−1)n/n!]

Main idea: Derangements are a direct application of inclusion–exclusion to the events “element i stays in its original position.”

Example: Calculating D5

There are 5!=120 total permutations.

Fix 1: C(5,1)4!=5·24=120

Fix 2: C(5,2)3!=10·6=60

Fix 3: C(5,3)2!=10·2=20

Fix 4: C(5,4)1!=5

Fix 5: C(5,5)0!=1

The number having at least one fixed point is

120−60+20−5+1=76.

Subtract this from all permutations:

D5=120−76=44

Equivalently, apply the derangement formula directly:

D5=120−120+60−20+5−1=44.

D5 = 44

n 0 1 2 3 4 5 6
Dn 1 0 1 2 9 44 265

Convention: D0=1. In particular, D1=0, D2=1, and D3=2.

4!−|L5|−|E6|+|L5∩E6|

Question: How many onto functions are there from X={1,2,3,4,5,6} to Y={a,b,c}?

Total functions = 36=729

C(3,1)26=3·64=192

C(3,2)16=3

Equivalent interpretation: Distribute 6 distinct objects into 3 distinct boxes with every box nonempty.

n even ⇒ Dn odd,    n odd ⇒ Dn even.

Pigeonhole Principle

If there are more pigeons than holes, then at least two pigeons must share a hole. Formally, if m pigeons occupy n pigeonholes and m>n, then at least one pigeonhole contains two or more pigeons.

Basic and Generalized Principles

Basic Pigeonhole Principle:

m>n ⇒ some hole contains at least 2 pigeons.

Generalized Pigeonhole Principle: For any distribution of m pigeons among n holes,

some hole contains at least ⌈m/n⌉ pigeons.

The corresponding lower-load statement is

some hole contains at most ⌊m/n⌋ pigeons.

Key word: The principle gives a guarantee that holds for every possible distribution. One example is not enough to establish a guarantee, while one counterexample is enough to disprove it.

Negating Counting Statements

Contradiction proofs for pigeonhole problems usually begin by negating the claimed occupancy condition.

Statement Symbol Negation
At least k ≥k <k, equivalently ≤k−1
At most k ≤k >k, equivalently ≥k+1
Exactly k =k ≠k, equivalently <k or >k

The quantifier must also change from some to every:

¬(“some hole has ≥k”)   ⇔   “every hole has ≤k−1”

¬(“some hole has ≤k”)   ⇔   “every hole has ≥k+1”

¬(“some hole has exactly k”)   ⇔   “every hole has ≠k”

Understanding Two Common Claims

Claim: At least two pigeons go into the same hole.

Meaning: There exists at least one hole containing two or more pigeons.

Negation: No two pigeons share a hole; equivalently, every hole contains at most one pigeon.

Claim: There is some hole containing at most 7 pigeons.

Meaning: There exists at least one hole containing 7 or fewer pigeons.

Negation: No hole contains 7 or fewer pigeons; equivalently, every hole contains at least 8 pigeons.

PHP Q1. Four Pigeons and Five Holes

Claim: At least two pigeons must go into the same hole.

The negation says that every hole contains at most one pigeon. This is possible because the pigeons may occupy four different holes.

Occupancies: (1,1,1,1,0)

This counterexample has no shared hole.

Claim is false

PHP Q2. Four Pigeons and Four Holes

Claim: At least two pigeons must go into the same hole.

Again, the negation is possible: place exactly one pigeon in each hole.

Occupancies: (1,1,1,1)

Therefore, sharing may occur, but it is not guaranteed.

Claim is false

PHP Q3. Four Pigeons and Three Holes

Claim: At least two pigeons must go into the same hole.

The negation would require every hole to contain at most one pigeon. Three holes could then hold at most 3 pigeons, which cannot accommodate all 4 pigeons.

4>3 ⇒ some hole has at least 2 pigeons.

Claim is guaranteed

PHP Q4. Six Pigeons and Four Holes

Question: Which of the following claims are guaranteed for every distribution of 6 pigeons among 4 holes?

Claim Guaranteed? Reason or counterexample
A. At least 2 pigeons share a hole Yes Since 6>4, the basic principle guarantees repetition.
B. Some hole has at most 2 pigeons Yes If every hole had at least 3, at least 4·3=12 pigeons would be needed.
C. Some hole has exactly 2 pigeons No Counterexample: (6,0,0,0).
D. At least 3 pigeons share a hole No Counterexample: (2,2,1,1).
E. Some hole has at most 1 pigeon Yes If every hole had at least 2, at least 4·2=8 pigeons would be needed.
F. Some hole is empty No Counterexample: (3,1,1,1).

A, B, and E are guaranteed true.

Fast Contradiction Method

To test whether a claim is guaranteed:

  1. Negate both the occupancy condition and its quantifier.
  2. Assume that the negation holds in every hole.
  3. Calculate the largest or smallest possible total under that assumption.
  4. If this conflicts with the given number of pigeons, the original claim is guaranteed. Otherwise, construct a counterexample.

¬(some ≥k)   ⇔   all ≤k−1

¬(some ≤k)   ⇔   all ≥k+1

¬(some =k)   ⇔   all (<k or >k)
