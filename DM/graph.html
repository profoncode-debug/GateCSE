Introduction to Graphs

A graph is a mathematical structure used to represent relationships.

  • Nodes (or vertices): represent the objects.
  • Edges: connect vertices and represent relationships between them. Directed edges are also called arcs.
02

Basic Terminologies in Graph Theory

2.1 Directed and Undirected Graphs

A graph is written as an ordered pair G = (V, E), where V is the set of vertices (objects of any kind) and E is the set of edges connecting vertices from V.

  • Unordered pair {a, b}: order does not matter; {a, b} = {b, a}. Example: {0, 1} = {1, 0}.
  • Ordered pair (a, b): order matters; for distinct a and b, (a, b) ≠ (b, a).

Remember: G = (V, E) is an ordered pair for both graph types. The distinction is whether the edges are unordered or ordered pairs.

2.2 Self-Loops

A self-loop is an edge that connects a vertex to itself. In a directed graph, it is written as (v, v).

  • Undirected graphs: following the slide convention, assume no self-loops unless specified otherwise.
  • Directed graphs: following the slide convention, self-loops are allowed unless specified otherwise.

Convention matters: whether loops are allowed depends on the graph definition. A simple undirected graph has no self-loops; always follow any explicit rule in the question.

2.3 Defining an Edge

  • Notation: for convenience, an undirected edge {u, v} is written as uv. Thus uv = vu denotes the same edge.
  • Incidence: if e = uv is an edge of G, then e is incident to u and v.
  • Endpoints: u and v are the endpoints of e.

Directed edges: order matters, so (u, v) denotes u → v and differs from (v, u) when u ≠ v.

2.4 Parallel Edges

Parallel edges (or multiple edges) are distinct edges joining the same pair of vertices; in directed graphs, they must also have the same direction.

  • Undirected: two or more distinct edges with endpoints {a, b} are parallel, since {a, b} = {b, a}.
  • Directed, same direction: distinct edges e1 and e2 both from a to b correspond to (a, b) and are parallel.
  • Directed, opposite directions: a → b and b → a correspond to (a, b) and (b, a). For a ≠ b, they are not parallel.

Notation: parallel edges are distinct (e1 ≠ e2) even though their endpoints match. To allow multiple edges, give them separate identities; a set of endpoint pairs alone cannot store repeated pairs.

2.5 Simple Graphs

A simple graph, as defined in these notes, is a finite undirected graph with:

  • No self-loops: an edge cannot connect a vertex to itself.
  • No multiple (parallel) edges: there is at most one edge between any two distinct vertices.

Default assumption: all graphs in these notes are simple unless stated otherwise.

2.6 Adjacent Nodes and Edges

Adjacent nodes (neighbours): two vertices are adjacent if there is an edge directly between them.

Adjacent edges: two distinct edges are adjacent if they share a common endpoint.

Non-example: e1 = bd and e3 = ac are not adjacent, as they have no common endpoint.

Remember: adjacency relates vertex to vertex or edge to edge; incidence relates an edge to its endpoint vertex.

2.7 Degree of a Vertex

In an undirected graph, the degree of a vertex v, denoted deg(v), counts the edges incident to v, with each self-loop counted twice.

  • Each non-loop edge incident to v contributes 1; each loop at v contributes 2.
  • Parallel edges, when allowed, are counted separately.
  • In a simple graph, deg(v) = |N(v)|, where N(v) is the set of neighbours of v. This equality need not hold when loops or parallel edges are present.

Loop example: if v has one non-loop edge and one self-loop, then deg(v) = 1 + 2 = 3.

2.8 Order and Size of a Graph

  • Order: the cardinality (number of elements) of the vertex set. |G| = |V(G)|.
  • Size: the cardinality of the edge set. ‖G‖ = |E(G)|.

Remember: order counts vertices; size counts edges.

2.9 Degree Sequence

The degree sequence of a graph of order n is the n-term sequence of its vertex degrees, usually written in non-increasing (descending) order. Include every vertex, even those of degree 0.

Degree sequence: (3, 3, 2, 2) — four terms because the graph has four vertices.

2.10 Handshaking Theorem

For a finite undirected graph G = (V, E) with m = |E| edges:

∑v ∈ V deg(v) = 2m = 2|E|

Why: each non-loop edge contributes 1 to the degree of each endpoint, so it is counted twice. A self-loop also contributes 2 at its single endpoint. Thus the theorem holds even with loops and multiple edges.

Example: for the graph above, 3 + 3 + 2 + 2 = 10 = 2 × 5, matching its 5 edges.

Q1. GATE CSE 2003 — Question 40: Minimum Degree

Question: a graph G = (V, E) satisfies |E| ≤ 3|V| − 6. Its minimum degree is defined as δ(G) = minv ∈ V deg(v). Which of the following cannot be the minimum degree of G?

  1. 3
  2. 4
  3. 5
  4. 6

Solution: let n = |V|, m = |E|, and δ = δ(G). Every vertex has degree at least δ, so the handshaking theorem gives:

nδ ≤ ∑v ∈ V deg(v) = 2m ≤ 6n − 12.

If δ = 6, then 6n ≤ 6n − 12, which is impossible. Equivalently, δ ≤ 6 − 12/n < 6.

Answer: D. 6 — the minimum degree must be less than 6.

Q3. GATE CSE 2017 Set 2 — Question 23: Maximum Vertices

Question: G is an undirected graph with n vertices and 25 edges such that each vertex has degree at least 3. Find the maximum possible value of n.

Solution: by the handshaking theorem, ∑v ∈ V deg(v) = 2 × 25 = 50. Since every vertex has degree at least 3:

3n ≤ 50 ⇒ n ≤ ⌊50/3⌋ = 16.

Achievable: start with a cycle on 16 vertices (16 edges), join each pair of opposite vertices (8 more edges), then add one unused edge. This gives 25 edges, with two vertices of degree 4 and the other fourteen of degree 3.

Answer: 16.

Watch out: “at least 3” means δ(G) ≥ 3, so use 3n ≤ 50; do not assume all degrees equal 3.

Q6. Edges After Vertex Deletion

Question: G has 10 vertices. Deleting each vertex in turn, together with all its incident edges, gives 10 vertex-deleted subgraphs. Their edge counts are 12, 12, 12, 12, 11, 11, 11, 11, 10, 10. How many edges are in G?

Solution: let e = |E(G)|. Deleting vertex vi removes deg(vi) edges, so the remaining edge count is ei′ = e − deg(vi).

Sum over all 10 deletions and use ∑i=110 deg(vi) = 2e:

∑i=110 ei′ = 10e − 2e = 8e.

The given counts sum to 4 × 12 + 4 × 11 + 2 × 10 = 112. Hence 112 = 8e.

Answer: e = 14 edges.

Shortcut: for a simple graph on n vertices, the sum of edge counts over all vertex-deleted subgraphs is (n − 2)|E|: each original edge survives every deletion except those of its two endpoints.

Q7. Maximum Vertices with 35 Edges

Question: what is the maximum number of vertices in an undirected graph with 35 edges if every vertex has degree ≥ 3?

Solution: let n = |V|. By the handshaking theorem, ∑v ∈ V deg(v) = 2 × 35 = 70. Since every degree is at least 3:

3n ≤ 70 ⇒ n ≤ ⌊70/3⌋ = 23.

Achievable: take a cycle with vertices 0, 1, …, 22 (23 edges). Add the 11 edges {i, i + 11} for i = 1, …, 11, then add {0, 2}. There are 35 edges; vertex 2 has degree 4 and the other 22 vertices have degree 3.

Answer: 23 vertices.

Q8. A Common Odd Degree Divides the Edge Count

Question: suppose every vertex of an undirected graph G has the same odd degree k. Prove that k divides |E(G)|.

Proof: let n = |V| and e = |E|. By the handshaking theorem, nk = 2e. Since k is odd and nk is even, n must be even. Write n = 2t, where t is an integer.

Substitute into nk = 2e: 2tk = 2e ⇒ e = tk.

|E(G)| is an integer multiple of k. Hence k ∣ |E(G)|.

Watch out: the hypothesis says all vertices have the same odd degree k, not merely that their degrees are odd.

2.11 Why Does an Undirected Graph Have an Even Number of Odd-Degree Vertices?

Every finite undirected graph has an even number of vertices of odd degree: 0, 2, 4, …

Proof: partition V into Veven (even-degree vertices) and Vodd (odd-degree vertices). By the handshaking theorem:

2|E| = ∑v ∈ Veven deg(v) + ∑v ∈ Vodd deg(v)

  • The total degree sum is even, since it equals 2|E|.
  • The sum over even-degree vertices is also even. Therefore, the sum over odd-degree vertices must be even.
  • A sum of odd integers is even only when the number of terms is even. Hence |Vodd| is even.

Remember: the number of even-degree vertices can be odd or even. Degree 0 is even, so isolated vertices belong to Veven.

Q2. GATE CSE 2013 — Question 25: Degree Statements

Question: which of the following statements is/are true for undirected graphs?

  • P: the number of odd-degree vertices is even.
  • Q: the sum of degrees of all vertices is even.
  1. P only
  2. Q only
  3. Both P and Q
  4. Neither P nor Q

Solution: by the handshaking theorem, ∑v ∈ V deg(v) = 2|E|, which is even. Hence Q is true. Removing the even-degree terms leaves an even sum of odd degrees, so the number of those terms must be even. Hence P is true.

Answer: C. Both P and Q.

Q9. Can 15 People Each Have Exactly Three Friends?

Question: using graph theory, determine whether each person in a group of 15 individuals can have exactly three friends within the group. Assume friendship is symmetric (it goes both ways).

Solution: represent each person by a vertex and each friendship by an undirected edge. Exactly three friends per person means every vertex has degree 3.

This would give 15 odd-degree vertices, contradicting the theorem that an undirected graph has an even number of odd-degree vertices.

Equivalently: the handshaking theorem requires 2|E| = 15 × 3 = 45, which is impossible because 2|E| is even.

Answer: Not possible.

2.12 Indegree and Outdegree

In a directed graph, count incoming and outgoing edges separately:

  • Indegree deg−(v): the number of edges entering v.
  • Outdegree deg+(v): the number of edges leaving v.
  • A self-loop at v contributes 1 to its indegree and 1 to its outdegree. Parallel edges, when allowed, are counted separately.

∑v ∈ V deg−(v) = ∑v ∈ V deg+(v) = |E|

Why: every directed edge has one source and one destination, so it contributes exactly 1 to each total. This holds even with self-loops and parallel edges.

Remember: total indegree equals total outdegree across the graph; an individual vertex need not have equal indegree and outdegree.

2.13 Walk

A walk is a sequence of alternating vertices and edges v0, e1, v1, …, ek, vk, where each edge ei joins the preceding and following vertices. We often write only the vertices.

  • Vertices and edges may repeat, but every consecutive pair must be joined by an edge.
  • Length: the number of edge traversals, counting repetitions. A walk with k edges has k + 1 vertex entries.
  • Closed walk: the first and last vertices are the same; otherwise the walk is open.
  • Trivial walk: a single vertex, with length 0.

2.14 Path

A path is a walk in which no vertex repeats. Consequently, no edge repeats either.

  • a → b → d → c: a path of length 3.
  • a → b → a: not a path, because a repeats.
  • a → b → d → c → a: not a path under this definition, because a repeats; it is a cycle.

2.15 Cycle

In a simple undirected graph, a cycle is a closed walk of length at least 3 with no repeated vertices except the first/last and no repeated edges.

  • a → b → d → c → a: a cycle of length 4.
  • a → b → a: a closed walk, but not a cycle in this undirected graph, since the same edge ab is traversed twice.

Walk: repetition allowed. Trail: no repeated edges. Path: no repeated vertices. Cycle: returns to its starting vertex, with no other vertex repetition or edge repetition.

2.16 Trail

A trail is a walk in which no edge repeats. Vertices may repeat.

Example: add a new vertex e and edge ae to the square graph above, giving E = {ab, bd, dc, ca, ae}. Consider:

a → b → d → c → a → e

  • Edges used: ab, bd, dc, ca, ae — all distinct, so this is a trail of length 5.
  • Vertex a repeats, so this trail is not a path.
  • a → b → a is not a trail in this undirected graph: ab and ba are the same edge.

Relationship: every path is a trail, and every trail is a walk. A trail can be open or closed; every cycle is a closed trail.

2.17 Connected and Disconnected Graphs

  • Connected vertices: two vertices are connected if there is a path between them.
  • Connected graph: an undirected graph is connected if every pair of vertices is connected.
  • Disconnected graph: at least one pair of vertices has no path between them.

Connected vs adjacent: adjacent vertices have a direct edge; connected vertices may be joined through intermediate vertices.

Crossing edges do not automatically form a connection. A crossing is a junction only if a vertex is explicitly marked there.

2.18 Maximally Connected Subgraph

A subgraph is formed using vertices and edges of G, keeping the endpoints of every chosen edge. A connected component is a maximal connected subgraph: it is connected and is not contained in any larger connected subgraph of G.

Maximal means it cannot be extended within G while remaining connected; it does not mean the component with the most vertices. Components can have different sizes.

  • Every vertex belongs to exactly one connected component. Components partition the vertex set; distinct components share no vertices.
  • There is no edge between different components; such an edge would connect them into one component.
  • An isolated vertex is a connected component by itself.
  • For a graph with at least one vertex: connected ⇔ exactly 1 component; disconnected ⇔ at least 2 components.

2.19 Distance Between Vertices

The distance between vertices a and b, denoted dist(a, b), is the length of a shortest path between them. In these unweighted graphs, path length is the number of edges.

  • dist(v, v) = 0 via the trivial path.
  • In an undirected graph, dist(a, b) = dist(b, a).
  • If no path exists, the distance is commonly taken as ∞.

Square example: in the earlier square with E = {ab, bd, dc, ca}, a → b has length 1, while a → c → d → b has length 3. Therefore, dist(a, b) = 1.

dist(a, e) = min {1, 2, 4} = 1. Choose a shortest path, rather than any available path.

2.20 Diameter of a Graph

The diameter of a finite connected graph is the maximum distance between any two vertices.

diam(G) = maxa, b ∈ V(G) dist(a, b)

Procedure: find the shortest-path distance for each pair of vertices, then take the largest of these distances.

Watch out: a triangle has paths of length 2, but those are not shortest paths between their endpoints. The diameter is not the length of a longest path.

More examples: the earlier square has diameter 2; the five-vertex graph in 2.19 also has diameter 2.

Disconnected graphs: using the ∞ distance convention, the diameter is ∞. Some texts leave it undefined; here, the diameter definition above is for connected graphs.

3.6 Hypercube Graphs

The n-dimensional hypercube, or n-cube, is denoted by Qn. Its vertices are all binary strings of length n.

Adjacency rule: two vertices are adjacent if and only if they differ in exactly one bit position.

For example, 00 and 10 are adjacent in Q2, but 00 and 11 are not. In Q3, 000 and 100 are adjacent. Drawing crossings without a marked vertex are not junctions.

Number of Vertices

Each of the n bit positions has two choices, 0 or 1. Thus the number of length-n binary strings is:

|V(Qn)| = 2n.

Degree of Every Vertex

From any vertex, flip one of its n bits to obtain a neighbour. The n choices produce n distinct neighbours, so every vertex has degree n. Hence Qn is n-regular.

  • If they differ in k positions, at least k edges are needed, since one edge changes only one bit.
  • Flipping those k differing bits one at a time gives a path of exactly k edges.
  • At most n positions can differ. A string and its bitwise complement differ in all n positions and attain distance n.

diam(Qn) = n. Every pair is connected by bit flips, and complementary strings are a farthest pair.

Isomorphic Graphs

4.1 Meaning of Isomorphism

Two graphs are isomorphic if their vertices can be put in a one-to-one correspondence that preserves adjacency. They have the same graph structure, even if their labels, shapes, and drawings differ.

G ≅ H if there is a bijection f : V(G) → V(H) such that
{u, v} ∈ E(G) ⇔ {f(u), f(v)} ∈ E(H).

Moving vertices or bending edges does not change the graph, provided the same pairs remain connected. A crossing is not a vertex unless it is marked as one.

4.2 Graph Invariants

A graph invariant is a property preserved by isomorphism. Isomorphic graphs have:

  • The same number of vertices.
  • The same number of edges.
  • The same degree sequence.
  • The same number of connected components.
  • The same number of cycles of each length k.

A mismatch proves non-isomorphism. Matching these checks alone does not prove isomorphism; establish a vertex correspondence that preserves all edges.

Complement of a Graph

5.1 Definition and Example

For a simple undirected graph G = (V, E), its complement G̅ = (V, E̅) has the same vertex set. Two distinct vertices are adjacent in G̅ exactly when they are not adjacent in G.

Keep the vertices; exchange edges and non-edges.
For each distinct pair of vertices, exactly one of G and G̅ contains its edge.

Here both graphs have vertex set {a, b, c, d}. Their edge sets are disjoint and together contain all six possible edges. The crossing in G̅ is not an extra vertex.

5.2 Number of Edges in the Complement

If |V| = n, the total number of possible edges between distinct vertices is n(n − 1)/2. Every such edge belongs either to G or to G̅, so:

|E(G)| + |E(G̅)| = n(n − 1)/2.
Therefore, |E(G̅)| = n(n − 1)/2 − |E(G)|.

Example above: n = 4 and |E(G)| = 3. Hence |E(G̅)| = (4 × 3)/2 − 3 = 3.

5.3 Self-Complementary Graphs

A graph is self-complementary if it is isomorphic to its complement: G ≅ G̅. The edge sets need not be identical; a relabelling preserves the structure.

In the example, G is the path a–b–c–d, while G̅ is the path c–a–d–b. Thus P4 is self-complementary.

Q11. Prove the Order Is 4k or 4k + 1

Question: let G be a graph isomorphic to its complement G̅. Prove that G has 4k or 4k + 1 vertices for some integer k.

Step 1 — equate the edge counts. Write n = |V| and e = |E(G)|. Isomorphic graphs have the same number of edges, so |E(G̅)| = e.

Using the complement formula: e = n(n − 1)/2 − e.

2e = n(n − 1)/2 ⇒ e = n(n − 1)/4.

Step 2 — use integrality. Since e is an integer, 4 divides n(n − 1).

Step 3 — use consecutive factors. One of n and n − 1 is odd, so it contributes no factor of 2. The even factor must supply both factors of 2; hence 4 divides n or 4 divides n − 1.

n = 4k or n = 4k + 1.
Equivalently, n ≡ 0 or 1 (mod 4).

This is a restriction on the order of a self-complementary graph; it does not mean every graph of such an order is self-complementary.

Q12. GATE CSE 1990 — Question 1-viii: Order Modulo 4

Question: a graph which has the same number of edges as its complement must have its number of vertices congruent to _____ or _____ modulo 4.

Solution: let n be the number of vertices and e the number of edges. Since the graph and its complement each have e edges:

As e is an integer, 4 divides n(n − 1). The two factors are consecutive, so one is odd; the even factor must be divisible by 4. Thus n = 4k or n = 4k + 1.

n ≡ 0 (mod 4) or n ≡ 1 (mod 4).

Bipartite Graphs

7.1 Definition and Partition Rules

An undirected graph is bipartite (or a bigraph) if its vertex set can be divided into two disjoint sets V1 and V2 such that every edge has one endpoint in each set.

  • If v ∈ V1, its neighbours can only belong to V2.
  • If v ∈ V2, its neighbours can only belong to V1.
  • V1 ∩ V2 = ∅: the parts do not overlap.
  • V1 ∪ V2 = V(G): every vertex belongs to one of the parts.

Every edge goes between the two parts. There are no edges joining two vertices within the same part.

7.2 Odd-Cycle Test

A graph is bipartite if and only if it has no odd-length cycle.
Equivalently, every cycle it contains has even length.

Why cycles must be even: following a cycle alternates between the two parts. Returning to the starting vertex therefore takes an even number of edges.

How to rule out bipartiteness: find one odd cycle, such as a triangle (length 3) or a 5-cycle. That single cycle proves the graph is not bipartite.

Graphs with no cycles also satisfy the no-odd-cycle condition. To establish bipartiteness directly, exhibit a partition in which every edge crosses between the two parts.

7.4 Degree-Sum Lemma for Bipartite Graphs

If a bipartite graph G has parts X and Y, then the sum of degrees in X equals the sum of degrees in Y.

∑v ∈ X deg(v) = ∑v ∈ Y deg(v) = |E(G)|.

Proof: each edge has exactly one endpoint in X and one in Y. It contributes 1 to each part’s degree sum. Therefore, summing degrees over either part counts every edge exactly once.

7.5 Complete Bipartite Graphs

A complete bipartite graph Km,n has parts A and B of sizes m and n, with every vertex in A adjacent to every vertex in B. There are no edges within either part. Here m, n ≥ 1.

Each of the m vertices in A has n neighbours. Each of the n vertices in B has m neighbours.

7.6 Proof: Hypercubes Are Bipartite

Recall that vertices of Qn are length-n binary strings, and adjacent vertices differ in exactly one bit.

Partition the vertices by parity:

  • X: strings with an even number of 1s.
  • Y: strings with an odd number of 1s.

Every binary string belongs to exactly one of these parts, so X ∩ Y = ∅ and X ∪ Y = V(Qn).

Now consider any edge. Moving along it flips one bit: either 0 → 1 or 1 → 0. The number of 1s changes by exactly one, so its parity reverses. Thus every edge joins an even-parity string to an odd-parity string.

No edge lies within X or within Y. Therefore Qn is bipartite.

Example: 000 has even parity and is adjacent to 001, 010, and 100, all of odd parity. Likewise, 110 has even parity and is adjacent to 010, 100, and 111.

Trees and Forests

Throughout this topic, graphs are finite, simple, and undirected, with n ≥ 1 vertices. An acyclic graph has no cycles.

9.1 Definitions and Examples

A forest is an undirected graph with no cycles. It may have one or several connected components, including isolated vertices.

A tree is a connected forest: it is both connected and acyclic.

Every tree is a forest. Every connected component of a forest is a tree.

9.2 Equivalent Conditions for a Tree

For a graph T on n vertices, each of the following is equivalent to T being a tree:

  1. T is connected and acyclic — the definition of a tree.
  2. Any two distinct vertices are joined by a unique path.
  3. T is minimally connected: it is connected, but removing any edge disconnects it.
  4. T is maximally acyclic: it has no cycles, but adding an edge between any two non-adjacent vertices creates a cycle.
  5. T is connected and |E(T)| = n − 1.
  6. T is acyclic and |E(T)| = n − 1.

Tree = minimally connected = maximally acyclic.
The statements concern removing or adding edges on the same vertex set.

9.3 Forest Edges and Connected Components

Let a forest have n vertices and c connected components. Each component is a tree. If component i has ni vertices, it has ni − 1 edges. Summing over the components gives:

|E| = ∑(ni − 1) = n − c.

  • Tree: c = 1, so |E| = n − 1.
  • Forest that is not a tree: c ≥ 2, so |E| ≤ n − 2.
  • Edgeless forest: c = n, so |E| = 0.

Theorem: Lower Bound on Connected Components

Let an undirected graph G have n vertices, m edges, and c connected components. Then:

c ≥ n − m.
Moreover, c = n − m if and only if G is a forest.

Proof of the bound: if component i has ni vertices and mi edges, its connectivity requires mi ≥ ni − 1. Sum over all c components:

m = ∑ mi ≥ ∑(ni − 1) = n − c. Rearranging gives c ≥ n − m.

Why equality forces a forest: equality in the sum requires every component to have exactly ni − 1 edges. Each component is connected, so each is a tree. Their union is a forest.

Conversely: a forest satisfies m = n − c, so c = n − m.

GATE takeaway: at least n − m components for any graph; exactly n − m components means no cycles.

Graph Colouring

Recall that two vertices are adjacent if they are directly connected by an edge. Here, graph colouring means vertex colouring.

12.1 Proper Vertex Colouring

A proper colouring of a graph G assigns a colour to every vertex so that adjacent vertices have different colours.

  • For every edge uv, colour(u) ≠ colour(v).
  • Non-adjacent vertices may receive the same colour.
  • Colours may be represented by labels such as 1, 2, 3; the labels themselves do not matter.

12.2 Chromatic Number and k-Colourability

The chromatic number of G, denoted χ(G) (chi of G), is the minimum number of colours needed for a proper vertex colouring.

A graph is k-colourable if it can be properly coloured using at most k colours. All k available colours need not be used.

G is k-colourable ⇔ χ(G) ≤ k.
If G is k-colourable, it is also (k + 1)-colourable. To prove χ(G) = k, show that k colours suffice and fewer than k colours cannot suffice.

12.3 Example: A Graph with Chromatic Number 3

The slide’s five-vertex graph is reproduced below. Vertex names a–e are added for reference; each vertex also shows its assigned colour number.

Two colours cannot suffice: a, b, and c form a triangle. Every pair in this triangle is adjacent, so its three vertices require three different colours. Thus χ(G) ≥ 3.

The graph is 3-colourable, 4-colourable, and 5-colourable, but it is not 2-colourable. Being 4-colourable does not mean its chromatic number is 4.

12.4 Chromatic Number of a Complete Graph

Question: what is the chromatic number of the complete graph Kn on n vertices?

In Kn, every pair of distinct vertices is adjacent. Hence no two vertices can share a colour: all n vertices need different colours. Assigning a distinct colour to each vertex also suffices.

χ(Kn) = n, for n ≥ 1.

12.5 Edgeless Graphs and Path Graphs

Edgeless graph En: no vertices are adjacent, so all vertices can use the same colour. For n ≥ 1, χ(En) = 1.

Path graph Pn: for n ≥ 2, alternate colours 1 and 2 along the path. Two colours suffice, and the presence of an edge makes one colour impossible.

χ(P1) = 1.
χ(Pn) = 2 for n ≥ 2.

12.6 Chromatic Number of a Cycle Graph

For a simple cycle Cn, n ≥ 3. Start at any vertex and alternate colours 1 and 2 around the cycle.

  • Even n: the last vertex receives colour 2 and the first receives colour 1, so the closing edge also has differently coloured endpoints. Hence χ(Cn) = 2.
  • Odd n: alternating two colours forces the last and first vertices to have the same colour, although they are adjacent. Thus two colours are impossible. Colour the first n − 1 vertices alternately and give the last vertex colour 3. Hence χ(Cn) = 3.

χ(Cn) = 2 if n is even; 3 if n is odd.

12.7 Chromatic Number of a Bipartite Graph

A graph is bipartite if its vertices can be split into two disjoint sets A and B such that every edge has one endpoint in each set. There are no edges within either set.

Give every vertex in A colour 1 and every vertex in B colour 2. Every edge then joins different colours, so every bipartite graph is 2-colourable.

G is bipartite ⇔ G has no odd-length cycle ⇔ G is 2-colourable.
Thus χ(G) ≤ 2 for a bipartite graph.

Why the converse holds: in a proper colouring using at most two colours, put vertices of each colour into a separate set. No edge can lie within a colour class, giving a bipartition.

  • At least one edge: its endpoints need different colours, so χ(G) = 2.
  • At least one vertex and no edges: one colour suffices, so χ(G) = 1. For example, E4 is bipartite with chromatic number 1.

A bipartite graph may contain even cycles; only odd-length cycles are forbidden. For instance, C4 is bipartite and C5 is not.

2-colourable means at most two colours. A bipartite graph has chromatic number exactly 2 only when it has at least one edge.

12.9 Greedy Colouring Theorem

For a finite simple graph G, Δ(G) denotes the maximum vertex degree, and δ(G) denotes the minimum vertex degree.

χ(G) ≤ Δ(G) + 1.
If the largest vertex degree is d, then d + 1 colours always suffice.

Proof using greedy colouring: consider the vertices in any order. For each vertex, choose a colour not already used by its coloured neighbours. It has at most Δ(G) neighbours, so at most Δ(G) colours are forbidden. Among Δ(G) + 1 available colours, at least one is free.

This gives an upper bound, which need not be the minimum. The number of colours used by a greedy colouring can depend on the vertex order.

  • Equality: for Kn, Δ = n − 1 and χ = n = Δ + 1.
  • Strict inequality: for C4, Δ = 2, but χ = 2 < 3.

12.10 Four-Colour Theorem

Every simple planar graph is 4-colourable.
If G is planar, then χ(G) ≤ 4.

This guarantees at most four colours; a planar graph may need fewer. For example, K4 is planar and needs exactly 4 colours, while C4 is planar and needs only 2.

If χ(G) ≥ 5, G cannot be planar. The converse does not hold: a non-planar graph can have chromatic number at most 4, as the next example shows.

12.11 Guidelines to Find Chromatic Number

  1. Check for edges: a graph with at least one vertex and no edges has χ = 1. If it has an edge, χ ≥ 2.
  2. Check bipartiteness: G is bipartite if and only if χ(G) ≤ 2, equivalently if and only if it has no odd-length cycle. If it is bipartite and has an edge, χ = 2.
  3. Find a lower bound: an odd cycle requires at least 3 colours; a complete subgraph Kr requires at least r colours.
  4. Find an upper bound: χ(G) ≤ Δ(G) + 1. If G is planar, also use χ(G) ≤ 4.
  5. Exhibit a proper colouring: if you use k colours and have proved that at least k are necessary, conclude χ(G) = k.

An odd cycle proves χ(G) ≥ 3; a proper 3-colouring is still needed to conclude equality.

Δ(G) ≤ χ′(G) ≤ Δ(G) + 1.

13.5 Edge Colouring of Cycle Graphs

For a simple cycle Cn, n ≥ 3 and Δ = 2.

  • Even cycle: alternate two colours around the edges. The closing pair also differs, so χ′ = 2 = Δ. Even cycles are Class 1.
  • Odd cycle: alternating two colours forces the first and last edges to share a colour at their common endpoint. Instead, alternate colours on the first n − 1 edges and use a third on the last edge. Thus χ′ = 3 = Δ + 1. Odd cycles are Class 2.

χ′(Cn) = 2 for even n; 3 for odd n.
C4, C6 are Class 1; C3, C5 are Class 2.

13.6 A Necessary Condition for Class 2

Every Class 2 simple graph has at least three vertices of maximum degree.

Contrapositive: if a simple graph has at most two vertices of maximum degree, it is Class 1, so χ′(G) = Δ(G).

The result is one way. Three or more maximum-degree vertices do not guarantee Class 2: C4 has four maximum-degree vertices, yet is Class 1.

Havel–Hakimi Algorithm

The Havel–Hakimi algorithm tests whether a list of non-negative integers can be the degree sequence of a finite simple undirected graph.

14.1 Graphic Degree Sequences

A sequence is graphic (or graphical) if some simple undirected graph has exactly those vertex degrees. The graph need not be connected.

For n entries, necessary conditions are 0 ≤ di ≤ n − 1 and an even degree sum. These checks can reject a sequence early, but passing them does not prove it is graphic.

14.2 Havel–Hakimi Reduction Theorem

Sort the sequence in non-increasing order: d1 ≥ d2 ≥ … ≥ dn. Set k = d1.

Remove d1, subtract 1 from the next k entries, and leave all other entries unchanged:

(d1, d2, …, dn) is graphic if and only if
(d2 − 1, …, dk+1 − 1, dk+2, …, dn) is graphic.

Interpretation: a vertex of degree k can be connected to k vertices with the largest remaining degrees in a suitable realization. Removing this vertex consumes one degree at each of those neighbours. The theorem guarantees that this reduction preserves the answer.

14.3 Algorithm and Stopping Conditions

  1. Sort the current list in non-increasing order.
  2. If every entry is 0, or the list is empty, accept: the original sequence is graphic.
  3. Remove the first (largest) entry k. If k is greater than the number of remaining entries, reject: there are too few vertices to supply k distinct neighbours.
  4. Subtract 1 from the next k entries. If any entry becomes negative, reject: the original sequence is not graphic.
  5. Re-sort the reduced list and repeat.

All zeros ⇒ graphic.
A negative entry or too few remaining entries ⇒ not graphic.

Remember: remove the largest entry itself, decrement exactly k other entries, and re-sort after each reduction. Equal degrees are allowed; “decreasing order” here means non-increasing order.
